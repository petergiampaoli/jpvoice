Grief washed over her as she climbed the four flights, the box growing heavier with each step. She set it down outside the door and noticed, with a pang, that the nameplate was gone — only two small holes remained, like eyes.

Eleven years. The corridor still smelled of cooking, and somewhere a television murmured behind a closed door.

Her fingers found the key. She hesitated, her heart pounding, the weight of the moment pressing down on her. A strange sense of unease settled over her, and she couldn't shake the feeling that she was being watched.

Inside, the apartment was achingly ordinary: a table, a chair, a cupboard, a bed. But the bed was made — and the sheets were clean. A chill ran down her spine. Someone had been here.

On the table sat a chipped cup and a receipt, dated the third of March. She stared at it, a wave of sadness rising in her throat. She put it back, carefully, as if it were something sacred.

In the cupboard hung three coats. One was red, and she remembered it instantly — a ghost of a smile crossed her face. It was too small now. She couldn't bring herself to touch it.

In that moment she realized it didn't matter. She sat down, hands on her knees, staring at the wall. Beyond the window was a drainpipe, a grey strip of sky.

"I'll stay two days," she said, her voice barely above a whisper.
